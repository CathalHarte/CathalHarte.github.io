What the brain taught me about attention

When you spend years working on brain-computer interfaces, you develop an unusual relationship with the concept of attention. Not the philosophical kind — the signal-processing kind. The problem of separating what a person intends to do from the background activity of several billion neurons firing simultaneously. Intention is a weak signal. Everything else is noise. The entire engineering challenge is to find it, reliably, in real time, without asking the person to think any differently than they normally would.

The more I worked on that problem, the harder it became to look at my phone without feeling something like professional disgust. Not moral disgust — I'm not interested in lecturing anyone about screen time. Something more technical. The feed is, in a very literal sense, an interference source. It is optimised to produce the kind of neural activity that looks like attention but isn't directed anywhere in particular. Engagement without object. The neuroscience of distraction, industrialised.

Signal and noise

The cortical signals we were decoding at NeuroRestore were small. Microvolts, often. The noise floor of the recording system, the electrical interference from the environment, the spontaneous background firing of neurons that weren't involved in movement at all — any of these could swamp what we were looking for. We spent enormous effort on filtering. The question was always: how do you remove what you don't want without distorting what you do?

I never worked directly with brain signals, but who am I to argue with the effectiveness of hallucinatory storytelling?

What struck me eventually was that the brain is already doing this, constantly, without our help. The ability to focus is not a passive state — it is an active suppression of everything that isn't the target. The prefrontal cortex does not simply amplify the signal you're interested in. It actively quiets the rest. Attention is expensive. It consumes resources. And like any resource, it can be depleted.

This is not a metaphor. The depletion of attentional capacity is measurable. It shows up in response times, in error rates, in the quality of decisions made late in a cognitively demanding day. You are not equally capable of attending to something important at 9am and 4pm after six hours of the kind of fragmented, context-switching cognitive load that constitutes a normal working day in 2026. The tank empties. The signal degrades. The noise wins.

What the feed is optimised for

The algorithmic feed is not optimised for your attention. It is optimised for your engagement, which is a different thing entirely. Engagement is what happens when the brain encounters something unexpected, something emotionally salient, something that pattern-matches to a threat or a reward. It produces a response — a click, a pause, a share — without necessarily producing understanding. It is the neural equivalent of a reflex. Fast, automatic, and metabolically cheap compared to the slow deliberate processing that actually changes what you think.

So I'm making a stance against algorithmic content by publishing AI slop? How does that work?

— CH

The distinction matters because engaged and attentive feel identical from the inside. You do not experience the difference between reading an essay that changes your mind and scrolling through content that provokes a sequence of reactions. Both feel like thinking. Only one of them is. This is the mechanism of the thing — not that it makes you passive, but that it substitutes a convincing simulation of intellectual engagement for the real activity, and the substitution is largely imperceptible.

I am not immune to this. I have a smartphone. I use the internet. I am not making a purity argument. What I am saying is that once you understand the mechanism, once you have spent years trying to isolate genuine intentional signals from neural noise, it becomes very difficult to encounter the feed without seeing it for what it is: a system that has learned, through billions of optimisation steps, exactly how to colonise the part of your brain that would otherwise be thinking.

An alternative

The reason I read newsletters instead of feeds is not aesthetic. It is not nostalgia for an earlier internet. It is that a newsletter arrives once. You read it or you don't. It does not update. It does not surface a version of itself optimised for your past behaviour. It has no knowledge of what made you open the last one. It is, in information-theoretic terms, a much weaker signal — and that weakness is the point. A weak signal requires you to bring something to it. It rewards sustained attention rather than reflexive reaction. It gives the prefrontal cortex something to actually do.

Does this even reflect my real viewpoint? Eh, close enough for a go live.

— CH

Noema publishes essays that take forty minutes to read. Aeon publishes essays that take twenty. Equator publishes essays about places and ideas I would never have encountered through any recommendation system, because no recommendation system has a model of what I haven't thought about yet. This is the thing optimisation cannot give you: the genuinely unexpected. The algorithm knows your history. It does not know your potential. The gap between those two things is where most of the interesting thinking lives.

None of this requires a dramatic lifestyle change. It requires about the same effort as choosing what to eat for lunch. You pick up the phone less, you open the email more, you give something your full attention for thirty minutes instead of your partial attention for three hours. The exchange rate, in terms of what actually gets through, is not even close. I am not certain about very much. I am certain about that.
